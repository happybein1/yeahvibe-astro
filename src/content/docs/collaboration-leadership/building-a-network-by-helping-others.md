## Networking without selling

Many people dread networking because it feels like selling: pitching yourself, collecting contacts, asking for favours. There's a calmer way. Be useful to the people around you, a little and often. Help freely, not as a trade. Over time, people remember who helped them, and support tends to come back, often from directions you didn't expect.

## 1. Offer five minutes first

When you meet someone or reply to a message, ask about them before you talk about yourself: **"What are you working on? Is anything stuck?"** If you can help in five minutes (an idea, a link, a name), do it.

You don't need to be an expert. Listening and asking good questions already helps someone think more clearly.

## 2. Go deep before you go wide

A few people you meet regularly are worth more than a hundred you met once. If you already know two or three people you enjoy talking to, suggest a small monthly check-in: **each person shares one goal for the month, and next time you see how it went.**

It costs little and helps everyone keep going. And it grows trust you can't build at a large event.

## 3. Introduce people to each other

When you notice that two people could help each other, connect them. **First ask both of them if they'd like the introduction.** An introduction nobody asked for can feel like a burden. If both say yes, send one short message: who each person is, and why you thought of them.

Both people will remember that you thought of them.

## 4. Share what you're learning

You don't have to reach out to everyone yourself. Share what you're working on and what you're learning, including what didn't work. For example, once a week, write one honest post or message to your circle.

Honest updates make it easier for the right people to find you.

## 5. Give a little more, quickly

When someone asks for help, give slightly more than they asked for, but keep it quick:

- **Asked for an introduction?** Write a short note they can forward.
- **Asked for feedback?** Give three clear, practical points, not a long essay.

Fast and useful beats perfect and late.

## Keep it light: set a limit

Helping can quietly fill your week. Set a simple limit, such as **30 minutes a week** for favours and introductions, and keep the rest for your own priorities. When a request doesn't fit, say so kindly (see [Saying No](/organizational-processes/saying-no/)). Helping a few people well is better than helping everyone badly.

## What comes back

Don't keep score. Some people will never return the favour, and that's fine. But many will: with advice, an introduction, honest feedback or support when you need it. And you'll feel less alone in what you're building. (See [Connection and Loneliness](/personal-effectiveness/connection-and-loneliness/).)
