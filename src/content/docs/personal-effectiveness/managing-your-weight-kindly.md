## Health first, not appearance

Weight is a sensitive subject. Many people have tried diet after diet, felt judged, or blamed themselves. This guide starts somewhere else: **your energy, your health and how you feel**, not how you look.

For people carrying extra weight, losing even **5 to 10%** of their body weight brings real health gains: lower blood pressure, better blood sugar, easier movement, often better sleep. In a large US study, people at risk of diabetes who lost about 7% of their weight through eating and walking cut their risk of developing it by more than half. You don't need to reach an "ideal" number for it to be worth it.

## Why diets usually fail

Most strict diets work for a few months, then the weight comes back. Studies that follow dieters for years find that most of the weight lost is regained within two to five years. That's not a lack of willpower:

- **The body fights back.** After weight loss, hunger hormones rise and the body burns a little less energy. It treats the loss as a famine.
- **Strict rules are hard to live with.** Cutting out whole food groups, counting everything, or eating very little works only as long as you can keep it up, and most people can't for long.
- **All-or-nothing thinking.** One "bad" meal feels like failure, and the whole plan collapses.

So the goal is not a diet you start and stop. It's **a few habits you can keep for life**.

## What actually helps

These are the habits with the best evidence. You don't need all of them. Pick two or three that suit you.

- **Sleep enough.** Short sleep raises hunger: in studies, people who slept too little ate on average about 300 to 400 more calories the next day. See [Sleeping Well](/personal-effectiveness/sleeping-well/).
- **Eat mostly real food.** In a careful experiment, people offered ultra-processed food ate about 500 calories a day more than when offered unprocessed food, without trying to, and gained weight within two weeks.
- **Protein and fibre at each meal.** Beans, lentils, eggs, fish, yoghurt, vegetables, whole grains: they fill you up for longer.
- **Watch what you drink.** Sugary drinks, juices and alcohol add a lot of calories without making you feel full. Water, tea and coffee without sugar are easy swaps.
- **Slow down.** It takes about 20 minutes to feel full. Eating without a screen, and putting your fork down between bites, helps you notice when you've had enough.
- **Move every day.** Movement alone rarely makes people lose much weight, but it is one of the strongest helps for **keeping weight off**, and it improves mood and sleep. See [Moving and Eating Well](/personal-effectiveness/moving-and-eating-well/).

## Make it easy

Habits stick when the healthy choice is also the easy one. See [Habits Improvement](/personal-effectiveness/habits-improvement/).

- **Shape your surroundings.** Keep fruit on the table and sweets out of sight, or out of the house. Use smaller plates.
- **Plan a little.** A shopping list and a few simple meals you like stop the "nothing at home, let's order" moments.
- **Change one thing at a time.** Start with the easiest step. When it feels normal, add the next.
- **Weigh yourself, calmly.** People who weigh themselves regularly, such as once a week, tend to keep weight off better. Treat the number as information, not a verdict. If it makes you anxious, skip it and notice how your clothes fit and how you feel instead.

## When food is comfort

Many of us eat when we're stressed, tired, bored, lonely or sad. That's normal: food calms us quickly, and sweet or salty snacks give the brain a small reward. The problem is that the feeling comes back, and the snack doesn't fix what caused it.

- **Pause and ask.** Before a snack, stop for ten seconds and ask: am I hungry, or am I tired, bored, stressed or lonely? Real hunger builds slowly and almost any food would do. Emotional hunger comes suddenly and wants one particular food.
- **Do what you actually need first.** If it isn't hunger, try the thing you need: a glass of water, a short walk, a few slow breaths, a break, or a message to someone. If you still want the snack after ten minutes, have it, sitting down, without a screen, and enjoy it.
- **Know your moments.** Comfort eating often happens at the same times: after a hard day at work, late in the evening, in front of a screen. Spotting your own pattern helps you plan for it, for example with a short walk after work or an earlier bedtime.
- **Make snacks a little harder to reach.** Keep sweets and crisps out of sight, or buy them in small packs. Keep easy alternatives at hand: fruit, nuts, yoghurt, tea.
- **Look after the cause.** Tiredness and stress drive most emotional eating. Sleeping enough and lightening your load often reduce snacking more than willpower does. See [Stress and Overwhelm](/personal-effectiveness/stress-and-overwhelm/) and [Why We Feel What We Feel](/personal-effectiveness/why-we-feel-what-we-feel/).

## The mind side

- **Kindness, not guilt.** A heavy meal or a lazy week is not a failure. Guilt often leads to more comfort eating. Just go back to your habits at the next meal.
- **Think in months, not days.** Weight goes up and down from day to day with water and salt. What matters is the direction over a few months.

## Medical help

- **See a doctor** if you have a lot of weight to lose, a health condition, or if you've tried for a long time without results. Some medicines, hormone problems and sleep apnoea can affect weight.
- **The new weight-loss medicines** (such as semaglutide and tirzepatide) can help people lose around 15 to 20% of their weight in trials. They are prescription medicines with side effects, and weight often comes back when they are stopped. They work best alongside the habits above, under a doctor's care.

## When eating feels out of control

If thinking about food or weight takes over your days, if you often eat large amounts and feel you can't stop, or if you restrict, skip meals or make yourself sick, please talk to a doctor or a local eating disorder helpline. These are health conditions, not weaknesses, and they can be treated.

## At the societal level

Weight is not only a matter of personal choice. Cheap, heavily marketed ultra-processed food, long working hours, little time to cook, and streets that aren't safe for walking all push in the same direction. Weight stigma also harms health: people who feel judged often avoid doctors and exercise. Personal habits matter, and so do the food and places we live with.
