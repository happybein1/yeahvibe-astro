## Feelings have a chemistry

Wanting something, feeling calm, feeling close to someone, feeling stressed: each of these goes together with chemical messengers in the brain and body. They are not the whole story, since thoughts, memories and circumstances matter too, but knowing the main ones explains why some habits make us feel better and others leave us empty.

Here are five that play a big role in everyday life. The picture is simplified: in reality they work together, and scientists are still learning how.

## Dopamine: wanting and motivation

Dopamine is less about pleasure itself and more about **wanting**: it pushes us to go after things that might be rewarding. It rises when we expect something good, and when we make progress towards a goal.

- **The trap:** phones, games and snacks give quick, easy rewards. The brain learns to chase them, and slower goals start to feel dull in comparison.
- **What helps:** break big goals into small steps you can tick off. Each small win gives a bit of the motivation you need for the next one.

## Serotonin: recognition and contentment

Serotonin is linked to a steady mood, patience and feeling that things are okay. It is also closely tied to **recognition**: feeling valued, respected and that you have a place among others. When people notice what you do, thank you or rely on you, you feel calmer and more confident.

- **The trap:** chasing recognition through likes and comparison. It rarely lasts, and comparing yourself with others often leaves you feeling worse.
- **What helps:** doing things that are useful to others, and noticing your own progress. Recognition also works both ways: thanking someone or telling them what you appreciate lifts them, and the relationship too.
- **The basics:** daylight, especially in the morning, regular movement, and regular meals and sleep also support it.

## Oxytocin: closeness and trust

Oxytocin goes with feeling close to people and safe with them.

- **What helps:** a hug, a real conversation, time with friends or family, caring for someone (even a pet), and kindness, given or received.

## Endorphins: relief and pleasure

Endorphins are the body's own painkillers, and they bring a feeling of relief and well-being.

- **What helps:** exercise (the "good tired" after a walk, a run or a swim), laughing, music and dancing.

## Cortisol: the stress signal

Cortisol helps the body react to pressure: it raises energy and focus so you can deal with a challenge. In short bursts, that is useful.

- **The trap:** when stress never switches off, cortisol stays high. That disturbs sleep, mood, memory and health over time.
- **What helps:** give stress an end. Slow breathing with a long out-breath, movement, time in nature, and enough rest. The guide on [stress and overwhelm](/personal-effectiveness/stress-and-overwhelm/) has practical steps.

## What this means for you

A good day often has a bit of each: a small win, feeling valued for something you did, some daylight and movement, a moment of real connection, something that makes you laugh, and time to switch off.

- **Notice the quick rewards.** If you reach for your phone or a snack every time you feel bored or tired, try a short walk or a message to a friend instead.
- **Build in the basics.** Sleep, daylight, movement and people are not extras. They are what your brain chemistry runs on.
- **Be kind to yourself.** Low mood or low motivation is not a character flaw. It is often a sign that some of these basics are missing.

This is general information, not medical advice. If low mood, anxiety or stress last for weeks or affect your daily life, talk to a doctor.
