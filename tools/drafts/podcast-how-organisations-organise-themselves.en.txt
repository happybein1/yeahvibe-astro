S: Hi Emma, thanks for having me.
E: Whether it's a company or a sports club, someone has to decide who does what. Where do you start?
S: With three questions. Who does what, who decides what, and how people work together across teams. A good structure makes the answers obvious. A poor one makes people wait, repeat work, or argue about who is in charge.
E: So how do organisations divide the work?
S: There are four common ways. By function, like finance, sales or IT, so experts work together. By product, customer or region, so decisions are close to the customer. A matrix, where people belong to a function and a project at the same time. And small teams that own one result from start to finish.
E: Which one is best?
S: None, on its own. Most organisations mix them. The rule of thumb is to organise around what matters most right now. If expertise and quality matter most, group by function. If speed and customers matter most, group by product or outcome.
E: Does the structure set the priorities?
S: Not really, it follows them. First, leadership decides what matters most and puts money and people behind it. Then the structure follows. A big, lasting priority may get its own team. One that cuts across teams may get a committee. But a team without budget or authority shows a priority on paper and changes little.
E: How many people can one manager lead?
S: Usually five to ten. Fewer, and you get too many layers and slow decisions. More, and people get too little support.
E: You say most friction comes from unclear decisions.
S: Yes, not from bad people. For each decision that comes up often, like budgets, hiring or priorities, make clear who owns it, who is consulted before, and who is informed after. And push decisions down to whoever has the best information, within clear limits.
E: Let's talk about committees. Useful, or a burden?
S: Both. A committee is useful when a topic really needs several views, or needs independence, like an audit committee. It works when it has a clear purpose in writing, five to seven members, and a chair who keeps it moving.
E: But you said one owner should decide. A committee is several people.
S: Right, so it needs a clear decision rule. Either the committee decides together, by vote or agreement, with the chair deciding if it can't agree. Or it advises one named person who decides. It goes wrong when nobody knows which of the two it is. Everyone gives opinions, but nobody decides.
E: Anything else?
S: Give it real authority, and an end date or a yearly review. Many committees outlive their purpose. Ask: if it disappeared tomorrow, would anyone miss it?
E: And who decides the structure itself?
S: The people at the top. In a company, the CEO and the leadership team, with the board approving big changes. In a club, the board, sometimes with a vote of the members. In public bodies, the law, ministers or elected councils. But the best changes aren't designed alone at the top. Ask the people doing the work where things get stuck, try the change, and review it after six to twelve months.
E: How do you know the structure needs work?
S: Decisions go back and forth, or everything goes up to the top. Two teams do the same work. Nobody owns an important problem. People spend more time in meetings than on the work. Even then, don't reorganise too often. Clearer decision rights and fewer, better meetings are often enough.
E: So, one thing to try this week?
S: Pick one decision that keeps getting stuck in your team or club, and agree who owns it.
